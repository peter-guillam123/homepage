About Daylight

Most people who read the Guardian do not read it all day. A lot of them come once, look at the homepage, and go. The homepage is not built for them. It is built for the people who come back every hour, and it shows them everything, all the time, in case something changed. For the once-a-day reader that is a wall.

Daylight is a sketch of a different answer. It takes the day as the unit, not the minute. It asks what happened since you left and what is worth your time, it picks a handful of pieces and says why, and then it stops. It is public because the argument is easier to have with a thing than with a slide. It is not a Guardian product and nobody has agreed to anything.

How it is made

Everything on the page comes from the Guardian's Content API, which already knows what the editors chose for each of the five fronts, what readers viewed most, what they read deeply rather than just opened, and every piece published in the last 24 hours. No Ophan, no personalisation, no editor sitting behind it. The only human curation is the one the fronts editors already do, which the page borrows.

The eight pieces in "Worth your time" come from a small score: editors' pick, deeply read, most viewed, with a bonus for running on three or four fronts and a cap of three per section. The score is in one file and the page prints the reasons, so nothing is hidden. The brief at the top is written by Claude when an API key is in place and stitched from headlines when it is not. The page says which, every time.

The strip at the top is the bit I like most. It is the last 24 hours of actual sunlight over London, New York or Sydney, worked out from the date and the latitude, with a bar for every 20 minutes showing how much the newsroom published, stacked by pillar, and the eight as numbered points. Two hundred and fifty pieces, eight points. That is the whole argument in one picture.

The page remembers when you were last here, which edition you want and which pieces you opened. All of that lives in your browser and the "What this page knows" button lists it. Nothing is sent anywhere. If you have your own Content API key you can paste it in and the page will fetch the day itself, live, in the browser.

It is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript with no build step, on GitHub Pages. A GitHub Action refreshes the data every hour. The world map is 4,852 dots worked out from a public outline of the continents. I described all of this and Claude typed it; I do not write code.

Diary

16 October 2026, afternoon The map moves, and Slow gets a Less

The map had ten cards around its edges and ten lines crossing to the pins, which read as a diagram of string. Chris's idea: keep the numbered pins so you can see where the ten are, and let one panel beside the map run through them, one at a time, on a loop. So that is what it does now. The map is half as big again, the panel on the right shows the number, the place, the section, the reading time and the headline, and one line points at the pin it means. Seven seconds each, a bar counting down, arrows if you want to drive, a pause when your mouse or your focus is on it, and a click on any pin jumps to that story. With reduced motion on, it stands still and the arrows do the work. Two fixes the same afternoon, both Chris's eye: the panel is a fixed height now, so the page no longer jumps as a long headline gives way to a short one, and the yellow line from panel to pin has gone, because the pin's pulse already points. The loops on the board and the map also used to stop whenever the mouse happened to be over them, which with a parked mouse and a scrolling page looked random, and on a phone one tap stopped them for good. Both now pause only while the mouse rests on the text, say "paused" when they do, ignore touch, and sleep when scrolled out of view or in a background tab.

Slow has a "Less" button on every piece now, like the wire's: less of this series, less of this section, fewer reviews or recipes or interviews. The page says how many it is hiding, and all of it lands on the same "Less of" list on Yours, with the wire's mutes, each saying where it applies and with a "Show again".

16 October 2026 Yours, a stamp, and a fuller post

Three small things, all Chris's. Every data page now carries the same stamp under its heading: "Updated 14:19 · 12 min ago · refreshes every hour", counting as the page sits open, and turning amber with "the hourly refresh is late" if it is. The board shows a full paragraph of each live blog post instead of a clipped sentence. And then the bigger one, below.

The three "What this page knows" dialogs were doing three jobs badly, and Chris asked the right questions: where do the mutes go to be undone, and where do kept stories go? One page now, "Yours", in the header of every page with a count of what you have kept. It is a reading list first: the pieces you kept, with their pictures, newest first, each with a "Let go". Keep is a button on every piece now, on Today's eight and its three doors, on the wire's articles and on everything in Slow, not only Slow. Then "Less of", every blog, subject and section you muted on the wire with a "Show again". Then the basics, edition included, and the field for your own API key. Then one button to forget the lot. Same promise as before: all of it is in your browser and nowhere else.

15 October 2026, night Slow

A third page, and a different pace. The wire is the Pret at your desk; this is sitting down with a friend. Features, interviews, recipes, reviews and picture galleries from the last couple of days, outside the news, one column of big pictures, newest first. Each piece arrives with its proper main image at a proper size, with the photographer's credit, and a gallery arrives with all its frames and captions in a strip you can swipe. Along the top, the series in the feed sit as a row of bubbles, "Country diary", "Pass notes", "Photos of the day"; pick one and the feed becomes just that series. Everything has a Keep button, and what you keep is listed in "What this page knows" with a way to let it go. Cards fade in as you reach them, unless you have asked your device for less motion. The page ends by suggesting you go and sit outside.

15 October 2026, evening The wireMilestone

A second page. Chris built something like it for the app a while ago and it never quite worked, so here it is again, built around the Content API and nothing else. The wire is news only, newest first: every news article from the last 24 hours and the latest posts from every news live blog, interleaved. There is a hierarchy. Articles on your edition's front run big, with a picture. Articles on another front, or deeply read, run medium with a small picture. The rest are a headline. Live blog posts show a taste of themselves, say which blog they are from, and are marked when the blog's writer flagged them as a key event. Four or more posts in a row from one blog fold into two and a count.

Every item has a "Less" button: fewer posts from this blog, less of this subject, less of this section. The page remembers in the browser, says how many things it is hiding, and lists them in "What this page knows" with a way back. The time rail on the left marks the hours and the point you were last here. It is the opposite of the first page in one way, it does not end, and the same in another: nothing is hidden about why anything is there.

15 October 2026, afternoon The brief looks at the front

Also: "From the other desks" is "From the other editions", and it now does what Chris asked in the first place. Each box is the top story that edition runs and no other front does, skipping anything already on the page. If a front has nothing of its own, the top story it runs that yours does not stands in, marked as shared.

The brief was summarising our own eight, which is circular. Chris's point: the three sentences should say how that edition's front saw the world at the moment the page was built. So the model now reads the news block at the top of the edition's front, in the order the editors placed it, with the latest events of any live blog among them, and writes from that. The page says which front and when. The eight below are still ours; the brief is theirs.

15 October 2026, later The map earns its space

The world map was a nice picture with a long list under it, which is not what the front of the Guardian Weekly looks like. It is now an annotated map: the ten stories sit as notes around the edge, three above, three below, two each side, each level with its pin where the stacking allows, joined by a thin leader line. Hover one and its pin and line light up. On a phone the notes become the list again, because ten notes round a small map is a mess. A stray line of dots across the Pacific turned out to be Fiji: its outline jumps the antimeridian and the land test read the jump as coast.

"Off the front" sharpened too, on Chris's steer. "Surprise me" became "Deeply read": a piece from any edition's deeply read list that is nowhere else on the page, longest first. "Further afield" became "From a series": the latest from the series people come back for, "A moment that changed me", "You be the judge", "Country diary" and the like, with reviews, newsletters and quizzes kept out. "A quieter piece" stays, minus obituaries.

15 October 2026 Subjects, not stubs

Two of Chris's notes from the morning. First, the strip: a chopped headline is a poor label ("That Kemi is spectacularly") and two pills could still land on top of each other. The words are now subjects, not stubs. When the hourly build has a Claude key, the model writes a two-to-four-word subject for each of the eight ("Gaza base", "Brand trial"); without it, the Guardian's own first tag stands in, and the headline cut is the last resort. The layout is two passes: every point gets its badge first, in whichever lane has most room, so nothing can overlap; then the words are handed out in rank order to those with space beside them.

Second, the board was showing blogs that had closed the previous afternoon under a heading that says "still moving". It now shows only blogs that are live now or closed within the last hour, and says so: "2 live now · 1 closed in the last hour". Every time is relative and keeps counting while the page is open: "4 min ago", "closed 2 min ago", "yesterday 21:55". When nothing is live the board says that, and shows the last one to close anyway.

14 October 2026, late Pills and a board

Two things were ugly and Chris said so. The words on the strip were white text with a black outline, which is what a meme looks like. They are now small pills: a numbered badge in the piece's pillar colour, a clean cut of the headline with no dots at the end, light or dark to suit the sky behind, laid out so none of them touch. When the right-hand edge gets crowded a pill keeps its number and gives up its words. "You were here" moved into the hour band.

The ticker's open-the-blog chips were a muddle, so the whole thing is one dark board now. The day's live blogs sit down the side as channels, with their subject, their colour and how many key events they have. The stage loops twelve events with a thin sun-coloured timer, and lights up the channel each one came from. Pick a channel and the stage becomes that blog's timeline. Pick it again, or "Back to the loop", and it goes back to turning.

14 October 2026, evening Second light

Chris looked at the first version and sent it back with notes, all of them right. What changed:

  • The strip now says what it is. The tiny ticks were every piece published, one pixel each, and read as static. They are now bars: how many pieces went up every 20 minutes, stacked by pillar, so you can see the newsroom's day against the sun. The counts sit inside the picture and the eight points carry a few words each where there is room. Before your last visit the strip is faded.
  • "Still moving" replaces the crawl. Twelve key events shared fairly across the day's live blogs, the newest from each first, one at a time on a slow board. Each is named by its subject in plain words with its colour, and a dot marks the blogs that are still live. The explanation went; the chips beneath are the blogs themselves.
  • A world map that does a job. Chris pointed at the map at the front of the Guardian Weekly: ten stories from around the globe, pinned where they happen. Daylight now does that automatically, from the Guardian's own country tags, one story per place and at most three per continent, the same whichever edition you read. The pins sit at capital cities, roughly, and the placing is only as good as the tags.
  • Five desks, not four. The Europe edition is in. The old four-column comparison became one short row: the top story each other desk is running that yours is not.
  • Smaller things. The date is the hero. The numbers down the left take their pillar colour. Thumbnails stay off phones.

14 October 2026 First lightMilestone

Started from a page of a notebook: a homepage for a less frequent audience, automated from metrics we already have, designed to combat overwhelm. Built the whole first version in a day. The decisions that mattered:

  • The page ends. "That's the day" is a section, not a joke. Infinite scroll is the problem as much as volume is.
  • Deeply read beats most viewed. The API exposes the "deeply read" list from the fronts, which is a better signal for someone with one visit to spend than clicks are. I had expected to need Ophan screengrabs for this and did not.
  • Every piece says why. If the choice is automated, the reasons have to be visible, or it is just another algorithm.
  • News is a ticker. Key events from the live blogs, as the writers marked them, rather than the blogs themselves.
  • The four fronts disagree. Putting them side by side on a map turned out to be the most interesting "who's reading what" we can do honestly without reader data.
  • Its own look. Warm paper, a serif with some character, one sun. It borrows the idea of pillar colours and nothing else.

Honest caveats: the fallback brief is dry, the surprise-me pool is only as good as the week's features, and the snapshot goes stale if nobody puts the key in GitHub. Also the top of the UK list this afternoon was a hair-loss column, because that is what readers stayed with. The data does not care about my taste, which is rather the point.
